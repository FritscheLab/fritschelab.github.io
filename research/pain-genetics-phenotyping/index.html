Research program

Nociplastic Pain: Phenotyping and Genetics

What shapes shared susceptibility across chronic pain phenotypes?

Our pain research focuses on nociplastic mechanisms and shared susceptibility across chronic pain conditions. We bring together statistical genetics, electronic health record (EHR) phenotyping, and patient-reported data.

Three mechanisms of pain

Nociceptive, neuropathic, and nociplastic pain describe different mechanistic contributions that can coexist and cut across clinical diagnoses.

Mechanism What it means
Nociceptive Pain arising from peripheral nociceptor activation in response to actual or threatened damage to non-neural tissue.
Neuropathic Pain attributable to a lesion or disease of the somatosensory nervous system.
Nociplastic Pain involving altered nociception when nociceptive or neuropathic mechanisms do not adequately account for the pain.

Definitions follow the International Association for the Study of Pain’s terminology.

Nociplastic mechanisms and overlapping pain conditions

Fibromyalgia and other chronic overlapping pain conditions motivate a view of pain that spans conventional diagnostic boundaries. Amplified sensory processing and altered pain modulation may contribute to these presentations alongside condition-specific mechanisms. This perspective, reviewed by Fitzcharles et al. (2021), informs our interest in shared and distinct genetic contributions across pain phenotypes.

Our work within the Chronic Pain & Fatigue Research Center

Our lab is part of the University of Michigan’s Chronic Pain & Fatigue Research Center, where we work with Dan Clauw and colleagues across pain research. Dan’s longstanding work on fibromyalgia and central pain amplification has helped shape our approach to nociplastic pain. His emphasis on mechanisms that extend beyond local pathology provides a conceptual foundation for studying pain across diagnostic categories. Dan and colleagues discuss this perspective in U-M’s overview of nociplastic pain.

Within this shared research setting, our lab contributes statistical genetics and EHR analysis, alongside our collaborators’ clinical and mechanistic expertise.

Where genetics and phenotyping fit

We are interested in how inherited variation relates to pain susceptibility and overlap across conditions. Phenotype definition is central to this work: diagnostic codes, reported symptoms, and longitudinal clinical records capture different aspects of pain.

We consider ascertainment, care patterns, and cohort differences when interpreting genetic associations. These questions connect our pain collaborations with the lab’s broader work in biobank research and statistical genetics.

2022

Genome-wide analysis identifies impaired axonogenesis in chronic overlapping pain conditions

Brain

Why it matters: Combining all chronic pain locations into one phenotype can obscure important biological differences. Genetic contributions were stronger for multisite than single-site pain; nine of 23 multisite loci replicated in HUNT, while functional genomics and brain imaging pointed toward axonogenesis and corticolimbic circuitry. The results also depend on how researchers define the pain phenotype.

2022

Unbiased immune profiling reveals a natural killer cell-peripheral nerve axis in fibromyalgia

Pain

Why it matters: No single assay is likely to capture all of the biology underlying fibromyalgia. Across patient cohorts, flow cytometry, blood transcriptomics, genetic analyses, and skin biopsy each pointed toward altered natural killer (NK) cell biology, including NK cells near peripheral nerves. Together, the findings support further study of a possible role for NK cells, but they do not show that NK cells cause fibromyalgia.
